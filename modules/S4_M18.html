Section 1

Wire Rope Construction and Terminations

How a Wire Rope Is Built

A wire rope is assembled from the inside out:

  1. Individual wires are laid together into a strand
  2. Several strands are laid around a core to form the rope

The core supports the strands and holds their shape under load:

  • IWRC -- independent wire rope core (stronger, more heat resistant)
  • Fiber core -- more flexible, less heat and crush resistant

Terminations and Efficiency

A termination is the end connection that lets a rope attach to a load or anchor -- an eye, a socket, or a fitting.

The termination is almost always the weakest point of the assembly. The rope body may hold its full strength, but the end connection decides how much of it you can use.

We rate terminations by efficiency: the percentage of the rope's strength the connection preserves.

Installing Wire Rope Clips

When U-bolt clips form an eye, installation is everything:

  • Saddle on the live side, U-bolt on the dead end -- "never saddle a dead horse"
  • Use the manufacturer's number of clips at the correct spacing
  • Torque the nuts to spec, then retorque after the first load

Reversing the clip (U-bolt on the live line) crushes the load-carrying part of the rope and destroys the connection's strength.

The Eye Splice

A hand-tucked eye splice in wire rope must have at least three full tucks of each strand back into the rope body.

  • Fewer tucks and the eye can pull out under load
  • Protruding strand ends are covered or blunted so they do not snag
  • The eye is sized so the included angle at the splice stays 60 degrees or less

Fiber rope splices require more tucks; the wire rope floor is three.

Wire Rope Sling Inspection and Removal

The Broken Wire Criteria

A rope lay is the length it takes one strand to make a full wrap around the rope. Within one lay, remove the sling if you find:

  • 10 randomly distributed broken wires in one rope lay, OR
  • 5 broken wires in one strand in one rope lay

The single-strand number is lower because clustered damage is more serious than the same count spread around. Anchored to ASME B30.9 and the sling requirements of OSHA 29 CFR 1926.251 and 1910.184.

Shape Damage: Kinks, Birdcages, Crushing

Damage to the rope's shape ends its service life, because distorted strands stop sharing load:

  • Kink -- a permanent tight bend that has distorted the rope
  • Birdcage -- strands sprung outward like a cage, from shock or sudden release
  • Crushing / flattening -- loss of the round cross section

Any of these is an immediate removal. The damage is permanent and cannot be worked back out.

Diameter, Corrosion, and the Core

Some of the worst wire rope damage is inside:

  • Reduced diameter -- signals internal wear, core failure, or corrosion; a removal condition
  • Corrosion -- rust pitting weakens wires and hides broken ones
  • Heat damage -- can ruin strength with no visible sign

Measure diameter against the original. The outside can look fine while the core is failing.

Heat and End-Fitting Damage

Two more wire rope removal conditions:

  • Heat exposure -- alters the steel and lubricant invisibly; treat strength as unknown and remove
  • Damaged end fittings -- cracked, bent, worn, or corroded sockets, thimbles, or eyes

Do not quench a hot sling with water -- it can worsen the damage. A heat-exposed sling has no safe remaining role.

Section 3

Alloy Chain Slings

Chain Grade and Identification

An alloy steel chain sling carries permanent, durable identification stating:

  • Size, grade, rated capacity, and manufacturer

Grade matters. Alloy grades (e.g., grade 80, grade 100) are the lifting grades. Lower-grade hardware chain (proof coil, etc.) is not a lifting chain.

If a chain cannot tell you its grade and rating, treat it as an unknown and keep it out of the lift.

Matched, Rated Attachments

Every fitting in the chain's load path -- master link, coupling links, hooks -- must be rated at least equal to the chain.

Makeshift fasteners are prohibited: no shop-made hooks, no field-bent rebar, no bolts or rods standing in for rated hardware.

A weak fitting silently downrates the whole assembly to itself.

Chain Removal Criteria

Remove an alloy chain sling from service for any of these, inspected link by link:

  • Wear beyond the allowed limit at any point on a link
  • Stretch / elongation -- a sign of overload; links get longer and thinner
  • Nicks, gouges, or cracks; bent, twisted, or stretched links
  • Heat damage or evidence of a repair by an unqualified party

Measure a set number of links against the original length to catch stretch.

Where Chain Shines

Alloy chain earns its place in the hardest service:

  • Heat -- handles temperatures that would destroy synthetics
  • Abrasion and sharp edges -- shrugs off what chews up other slings
  • Heavy, dirty, repetitive work -- foundries, mills, heavy fab

The trade-off is weight -- chain is the heaviest sling to handle. And it still gets the same inspection discipline.

Synthetic Web and Roundslings

Web Sling Construction

On a synthetic web sling, the eyes and end fittings are formed by stitching only, laid in an even pattern with enough stitches to develop the sling's full breaking strength.

The stitching is structural. Broken or worn stitches are a removal condition.

No staples, glue, or clips -- stitching is the only approved method.

Web Sling Removal Criteria

Remove a synthetic web sling from service for any of these:

  • Acid or caustic burns
  • Melting or charring of any part of the surface
  • Snags, punctures, tears, or cuts
  • Broken or worn stitches
  • Distorted or damaged end fittings
  • Missing or illegible identification

Roundslings and the Warning Core

An endless roundsling carries its load on hidden core yarns inside a woven cover. The cover takes the wear; the core carries the load.

Many roundslings use a brightly colored core or warning yarn. If you can see the core color through a cut or tear, the cover is breached and the sling is out of service.

You cannot inspect the core directly -- so a damaged cover means an unverifiable sling.

The One Rule Above All Types

Across every sling family -- wire rope, chain, web, roundsling -- one rule governs:

A sling with missing or unreadable identification is out of service, no matter its type or apparent condition.

Without the tag you cannot confirm rating, grade, material, or history. A sling you cannot identify is a sling you cannot trust.

Protecting Every Sling on an Edge

Sharp edges threaten every sling type at once:

  • They cut synthetic web and roundslings
  • They nick and kink wire rope
  • They nick and gouge chain links

One corner protector or softener over the edge protects whatever sling is on it -- and opens the bend radius. Keep them on the truck.
