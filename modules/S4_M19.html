Section 1

Shackles

Two Shackle Body Shapes

Shackles come in two body shapes for two kinds of loading:

  • Anchor (bow) shackle -- wide, rounded bow; handles angled and multi-leg loads
  • Chain (D) shackle -- narrow, straight body; for in-line, single-leg pulls

When sling legs come into a shackle at a spread, use the bow. Governed by ASME B30.26.

Two Pin Types

Two pin types, for two situations:

  • Screw-pin shackle -- pin threads in by hand; quick, for non-permanent connections you check each use
  • Bolt-type (safety) shackle -- bolt, nut, and cotter pin; cannot back out; for permanent installs or when the load may rotate

A rotating load can unscrew a screw pin. If the load may turn, or you cannot re-check the pin, use the bolt type.

Loading a Shackle Correctly

A shackle earns its full rating only under the right loading:

  • In-line -- the pull runs down the shackle's centerline
  • Symmetric -- balanced, not cocked to one side
  • Sling legs within an included angle of about 120 degrees

Spread the legs past 120 degrees, or pull off to the side, and you must reduce the rating.

Shackle Removal Criteria

Remove a shackle from service for any of these:

  • 10 percent loss of the catalog dimension at any point on bow or pin
  • A pin that will not seat flush -- shoulder not drawn down to the body
  • Bending, twisting, stretching, elongation, cracks, deep nicks or gouges
  • Excessive corrosion or pitting, or any unauthorized welding
  • Missing or illegible rating

Section 2

Hooks

The Latch Is Not a Load Point

A safety latch -- or a mousing when there is no latch -- bridges the throat to keep the sling or load from jumping out.

The latch (or mouse) is not a load-bearing part. Never rig so the latch takes weight. It retains the connection; the hook body carries the load.

Knowledge Check

The safety latch on a lifting hook is intended to:

Load in the Saddle, Never the Tip

The load belongs in the saddle -- the bowl at the bottom of the hook -- seated in line with the shank.

Wrong connections that reduce capacity or bend the hook:

  • Tip / point load -- load on the tip; pries the hook open, cuts capacity sharply
  • Side load -- pull across the hook
  • Back load -- load across the back, away from the throat

Hook Removal Criteria

Remove a hook from service for any of these:

  • Throat opening increase over about 5 percent (never more than 1/4 inch)
  • Wear exceeding about 10 percent of the original dimension at any point
  • Any twist from the plane of the unbent hook
  • Cracks, nicks, or gouges
  • Missing or illegible manufacturer identification; a damaged latch

Measure the throat against reference marks to catch spread early.

Section 3

Eyebolts and Hoist Rings

Shoulder vs Non-Shoulder Eyebolts

Two eyebolt types, and the difference is everything:

  • Non-shoulder (plain) -- no collar; vertical, in-line loads only. At an angle it bends or breaks.
  • Shoulder -- has a collar that braces side force; can take angular loads (with derating)

A plain eyebolt loaded at any angle is a failure waiting to happen. Angular load requires a shoulder eyebolt -- or better, a hoist ring.

Setting a Shoulder Eyebolt for an Angle

To use a shoulder eyebolt at an angle, all of these must be true:

  • The shoulder seats flush against the load, in full contact
  • The plane of the eye lines up with the direction of pull
  • The bolt is tightened / torqued to spec, not backed off

An eyebolt "turned to the side" loses capacity and can fail. Angular loading still derates it steeply -- consider a hoist ring.

The Swivel Hoist Ring

A swivel hoist ring bolts down like an eyebolt but its bail swivels 360 degrees and pivots to align with the load line.

Because it always pulls in-line with itself, its rated capacity is not reduced at an angle -- unlike an eyebolt, which sheds capacity as the pull comes off vertical.

It must be torqued to the manufacturer's spec and inspected like any other hardware.

Section 4

Turnbuckles, Links, and Rings

What a Turnbuckle Does

A turnbuckle is a length and tension adjuster: a body with two threaded ends (one right-hand, one left-hand thread). Turning the body draws both ends in or lets them out.

End types: jaw, eye, and hook.

Lock the turnbuckle against rotation (jam nuts or wire) so vibration cannot loosen it. It is an adjuster in the line, not a lifting pick point.

Knowledge Check

A turnbuckle's purpose in a rigging assembly is to:

Turnbuckle End Types

The three end types are not equal:

  • Jaw and eye ends -- closed; secure under load and slack
  • Hook end -- can open or unload if the line goes slack; weakest and lowest-rated

For overhead or critical work, use jaw or eye ends. Avoid relying on a hook end where it could come unhooked.

Limits on Turnbuckles, Links, and Rings

These load-path components share a few hard rules:

  • Never side-load -- the pull runs straight through, not across
  • Never exceed the rating
  • Never repair by welding -- heat destroys strength and hides cracks
  • Inspect for bent rods, stretched or cracked fittings, thread damage, wear

Master Links and Rings

A master link sits at the top of a multi-leg assembly, where all the leg loads gather.

It must be rated to carry the full assembly load through all its legs -- not just one leg's share -- and it must be marked with its rating.

Connecting links and weldless rings likewise must be rated to match the assembly and used only as manufactured, rated components.

Rig to the Marked Working Load Limit

Across every component in this module, the rating you rig to is the working load limit marked by the manufacturer on that specific piece.

The whole assembly is only as strong as its lowest-rated component. A heavy sling through a light shackle is limited by the shackle.

Knowledge Check

Across every piece of rigging hardware -- shackles, hooks, eyebolts, hoist rings, turnbuckles -- the rating you rig to is:

Unmarked Hardware Is Out of Service

The identity rule that governs slings governs hardware too:

A shackle, hook, eyebolt, hoist ring, link, or ring with no legible identification or rating is out of service until it is properly identified.

No marking means no known rating, grade, or history -- and no lift.

The 120-Degree Leg Limit

Tying the loading rules together: a shackle carries its full rating only when loaded symmetrically with the sling legs within an included angle of about 120 degrees.

Spread the legs past 120 degrees and the force on the shackle climbs -- reduce the rating, just as you would for a sling at a wide angle.
