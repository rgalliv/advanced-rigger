## Slide 1

Welcome to Center of Gravity Determination, the first module in the Advanced Rigger stage. Here is why this module comes first. Every rigging decision you make downstream -- what sling to grab, what hitch to use, where to hook up, how much tension ends up in each leg -- all of it assumes you know two things about the load. What it weighs, and where it balances. Get the weight wrong and you overload something. Get the balance point wrong and the load moves on its own the second it leaves the ground. I have been on jobs where a crew nailed the sling selection, nailed the angles, did everything by the book, and still had a skid spin a quarter turn on lift-off because nobody checked where the weight actually sat. [PAUSE / ASK] Before we start, ask yourself how you would find the balance point of a loaded pump skid sitting in front of you right now.

## Slide 3

First section. Before we calculate anything, we need to be precise about what this point is, because most people carry around a fuzzy version of it that works fine until it does not. We are going to define it, then look at two things it controls: which way the load swings, and whether the load hangs steady or wants to roll over. Those two behaviors account for most of the load control problems you will see in the field. I have watched a seasoned crew get surprised by a load that rolled in the slings, and every one of them could have told you what center of gravity meant if you asked. [PAUSE / ASK] Ask yourself whether you could explain the difference between where a load balances and where its middle is.

## Slide 4

Here is the definition, and it is worth getting exactly right. The center of gravity is the one point where the weight of the load is balanced in every direction at once. Not the middle of its outline. Not the heaviest component. The balance point. Two consequences fall straight out of that definition. First, if you could support the load at that point it would sit there without wanting to rotate. Second, and this is the one that matters on the hook, a suspended load will always rotate until that point ends up directly under the hook. It does not ask permission and it does not wait. The other thing to understand is that this point belongs to the load. It was there before you showed up. Your rigging does not create it or move it -- your rigging either lines up with it or fights it. On a job in a plant shutdown I watched two riggers argue for ten minutes about where to hook a gearbox, and the gearbox already knew the answer. [PAUSE / ASK] Say the definition back in your own words before you move on.

## Slide 5

This trips people up the first time they meet it. The balance point does not have to land on any actual metal. Think about a wedding ring, or a big steel flange ring, or a horseshoe. The balance point of a ring is the middle of the hole, and there is nothing there. Same story with a U-shaped weldment or an open skid frame. The point sits in the air the shape surrounds. Why does that matter to you practically? Because you will hear people say mark the CG on the load, and sometimes you physically cannot. What you can usually do is mark the vertical line the balance point falls on, on the outside of the load where you can see it, and that is enough to line your hook up. I have seen a fabrication shop paint a stripe down the side of every large weldment for exactly this reason, and the riggers loved them for it. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture a horseshoe and put your finger where it balances.

## Slide 6

This one is about the definition, and the definition matters because everything else in this module builds on it. The center of gravity is the single point where the weight of the load is balanced in every direction. Hang the load from that point and it sits still. Some folks will reach for the heaviest spot on the load, and that is close but wrong -- a dense motor on one end pulls the balance point toward it, but the balance point is not the motor itself, it is somewhere between the motor and everything else. Someone might think it is the geometric middle of the outline, and that is only true when the material is spread out evenly, which on real equipment it almost never is. Some people will point at the sling attachment points, but those are where you decided to hook up. The load does not care where you hooked up. The balance point is set by the load itself, before you ever showed up with a sling. [PAUSE / ASK] Take a second on this before you answer. Make your call.

## Slide 7

This is the operating rule that everything else serves. A load hanging on a line settles with its balance point under the hook, every time, without exception. So your whole job when you hook up is to put the hook over that point before the load leaves the ground, because if you do not, the load will do the moving itself. When that hookup is right, the load comes up level and it hangs still and it is boring, and boring is exactly what you want. When it is wrong, the load swings and rotates the moment it goes free, and there is a second problem riding along with it -- the legs stop sharing evenly. One leg picks up more than you planned. There is a related point about the load line itself needing to hang plumb. Dragging a load sideways puts stresses into the machine that were never in the design. On a warehouse job I watched an operator try to skid a crate sideways with an overhead crane and put a flat spot in a sheave doing it. [PAUSE / ASK] Think about the last load you saw picked and whether it hung quiet.

## Slide 8

The center of gravity is a point in space, not a spot you can put your thumb on. On a big steel ring, on a U-shaped weldment, on a pipe spool bent back on itself, that balance point sits in the open air inside the curve. Some folks will insist it has to land on solid material, and that feels right until you picture a donut -- the balance point of a donut is the hole. Someone might think weight has something to do with it, but a five hundred pound ring and a fifty thousand pound ring both balance at the center of the circle. Some people will tie it to the number of materials, and that changes where the point lands but not whether it can sit in open air. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture a horseshoe in your head and ask yourself where it balances. Make your call.

## Slide 9

Let us be concrete about what goes wrong, because swing sounds mild until you see it. First, the load accelerates sideways as it comes free. It is not drifting, it is being pulled by gravity into a new position, and a big load carries real energy doing that. Second, it rotates. Third, and this is the one that does not announce itself, the legs stop sharing the way you calculated. You sized those slings assuming a share, and now one of them is carrying more. Fourth, your tag line people go from guiding the load to reacting to it, which is how people get pulled off balance or dragged. And the sum of all that is a struck-by exposure for anybody standing in the path. I was on a precast job where a wall panel swung about three feet on lift-off because the hookup was off, and the closest laborer to it had done everything right and still had to move. [PAUSE / ASK] Where do people usually stand on your jobs during a pick?

## Slide 10

Gravity is going to win this argument every single time. A suspended load will always come to rest with its balance point directly beneath the hook. If you did not hook up over that point, the load moves itself to get there, and it does that the instant it leaves the ground. That movement is a swing, and a swing on a heavy load is how people get pinned. Some folks will say the load just gets heavier, but the weight never changed -- what changed is where the weight is hanging from. Someone might think the height matters, that it is safe until the load is up high, but the swing happens at the moment the load goes free. Some people assume the slings still share evenly, and that is exactly backward -- an off-center hookup is one of the main ways one leg ends up carrying far more than its share. [PAUSE / ASK] I have watched a crew lift a pump skid two inches and jump back when it spun a quarter turn on them. Nobody got hurt that day. Make your call.

## Slide 11

Now the vertical question, and this is the one that produces the ugly surprises. It is not just about lining up left and right. It is about whether the point you are picking from sits above or below the balance point. Above the balance point, the load is a pendulum. Nudge it and it swings back to level on its own. That is stable and that is what you want. Below the balance point, you have a pencil standing on its tip. The load wants to roll, and it will roll until the heavy part gets underneath the points it is hanging from, and it does not do that gently. There is a third case worth naming: pick points exactly level with the balance point. That is neutral. The load has no reason to come back to level, so wherever a bump leaves it, it stays. Think about a tall electrical cabinet with lifting lugs down low on the sides. That is the unstable case, and where the standards and the manufacturer allow it at all, it comes with tag lines and a deliberate plan. I have seen a tall cabinet rigged from low lugs roll about twenty degrees in the slings and everybody's stomach dropped at once. [PAUSE / ASK] Think of a load on your job with attachment points down low.

## Slide 12

This is the stability rule and it is the one that separates a load that hangs quiet from a load that rolls over on you. When the point you are picking from is above the balance point, the load behaves like a pendulum -- push it and it swings back to level. When the pick point is below the balance point, the load is balanced like a pencil standing on its tip, and it will roll to get the heavy part underneath. Some folks will say level is fine, but level is neutral -- the load has no reason to come back, so any bump leaves it wherever it ended up. Someone might think below is correct because it sounds like you are cradling the load, but that is the tip-over case. Some people will chase the heavy end, which is about where, not about how high. [PAUSE / ASK] Think about a tall electrical cabinet rigged from lugs down low on its sides. Make your call.

## Slide 13

Section two, and this is where we get to work. Four ways to find the balance point, in order of how much you should trust them. Documents first. Geometry when the load is simple and uniform. The moment method when the load is made of parts, which is most real equipment. And weighing when you need hard numbers and the load is sitting somewhere you can get scales under it. You will use all four, sometimes on the same job. What I want you to walk away with is the habit of asking which one applies before you start measuring anything. I have watched a rigger spend forty minutes calculating a skid that had the weight and balance point stamped on a plate at the other end. [PAUSE / ASK] Ask yourself which of these you have actually done.

## Slide 14

Four sources, ranked. Start with documents. If the people who built it published the weight and the balance point, that beats anything you are going to work out with a tape measure, and it takes thirty seconds to look. Second, calculation. Geometry when the load is one uniform material in a regular shape, the moment method when it is made of parts. Both are trustworthy, and both are only as good as the numbers you feed them. Third, weighing. Scales give you the load as it actually sits today, including whatever is inside it that nobody mentioned. Fourth, trial lift. And I want to be careful here -- the trial lift is verification, not determination. It tells you whether you were right. It does not do the homework for you. Now, whose job is this? Under B30.5 the rigger is responsible for making sure the load weight and approximate balance point have been obtained. That sits on you. On a refinery turnaround I watched a rigger stop a pick and go find the vessel drawings, and it cost twenty minutes and saved a bad afternoon. [PAUSE / ASK] Who typically supplies the weight on your jobs?

## Slide 15

There is an order of confidence here, and you always start at the top of it. Documented data from the people who built the thing beats anything you can work out standing next to it. A data plate, a general arrangement drawing, a bill of lading -- those are the first stop, every time. Some folks jump straight to calculating from dimensions, and that is a real skill you will use constantly, but it is what you do when the documents are not there, not instead of looking. Someone might lean on the operator, and a good operator has judgment worth listening to, but judgment is not data and it is not the operator's job to supply the weight. Some people will name the trial lift, and that is a verification step -- it confirms what you already calculated, it does not replace the homework. [PAUSE / ASK] On a plant job I have seen the weight stamped right on the skid frame while three people argued about it. Make your call.

## Slide 16

The easy case first, and it really is easy. One material, even thickness, regular shape -- the balance point is the geometric middle. A rectangular plate balances at the center in both directions. A length of uniform pipe balances at mid-length on its axis. A solid block balances at the center in all three directions. Simple. But here is what I actually want you to take from this slide, and it is not the easy part. It is knowing precisely when you have left the easy case. Torch a hole in that plate and the balance point moves away from the hole. Weld a bracket on one end and it moves toward the bracket. Taper the section, thicken one end plate, add internal bracing, leave product inside -- every one of those moves the point. On real equipment, the uniform case is the exception, not the rule. I have seen riggers treat a fabricated tank like a uniform cylinder and forget it had a dished head on one end and a flat on the other. [PAUSE / ASK] Look around and find something near you that is genuinely uniform.

## Slide 17

When material is spread out evenly and the shape is regular, the balance point and the geometric center are the same place. A rectangular plate of one thickness, one material, no cutouts -- measure the middle in both directions and you have it. This is the easy case, and it is worth knowing precisely so you recognize when you have left it. Some folks will hunt for a thickest corner, but the premise already told you the thickness is uniform. Someone might reach for a one third rule, and there are rules like that for triangles and other shapes, but they do not apply to a rectangle. Some people will say you cannot know without weighing, and weighing gives you the weight -- it does not tell you where the balance point sits. [PAUSE / ASK] Now ask yourself what changes the moment somebody torches a hole in that plate. Make your call.

## Slide 18

This is a method your Stage 2 work introduced, and it earns its place in the advanced course because it is the shop rigger's tool. When you can actually pick a piece up and let it hang free, the load will tell you where it balances without any arithmetic at all. You hang it from one point, let it stop swinging, and drop a plumb line straight down from the hook. The balance point is somewhere on that line -- guaranteed, because a hanging load always settles with its balance point under the hook. You mark that line right on the load. Then you hang it from a different point, drop a second line, mark that one too, and where the two marks cross is your balance point in two dimensions. A couple of honest limits. The load has to hang completely free -- if a corner is still kissing the ground or a wall, the reading lies to you. The hoist has to be rated for the whole load, because there are no shortcuts on a temporary hang. And wind will push a suspended load around, so this is an indoor, calm-air method. That is why it lives in the shop. I have watched a fab shop hang a big irregular weldment twice and paint the crossing point on it before it ever shipped, and the field crew loved them for it. [PAUSE / ASK] Where on your work could you hang a piece free before it goes out?

## Slide 19

The plumb-bob method reads the balance point straight off a hanging load. You suspend the load from one attachment point, let it settle, and drop a plumb line from the hook straight down. The balance point sits somewhere on that line, because a hanging load always puts its balance point under the hook. Mark the line on the load. Then you hang it from a different point and drop a second line, and where the two lines cross is your balance point. Some folks will reach for the weighing method, and that is a different valid tool -- it gives you the balance point from scale readings, not from a hanging line. Someone might describe measuring and dividing by density, which gets you the weight, not where it balances. Some people will name the moment method, which is the calculation you run on paper, not the thing you do with the load hanging in front of you. [PAUSE / ASK] I have watched a fab shop hang a big weldment twice and paint the crossing point right on it before it ever went to the field. Make your call.

## Slide 20

Here is the idea behind the method, and if you get the idea the arithmetic is nothing. A moment is a turning effect, and it is weight times distance from wherever you decide to measure from. That is it. The seesaw is the picture I want in your head. A small kid way out at the end balances a big kid sitting close to the middle. Neither weight alone nor distance alone explains that. What explains it is the product of the two. Now scale that up. A load made of several components is just several kids on the same seesaw, each one with a weight and a distance. The balance point of the whole thing is the spot where all those turning effects cancel out. That is what we are solving for. One thing to watch: weight times distance gives you pound-feet. If you ever find yourself adding pounds to feet, stop, because the units are telling you the method went sideways. I taught this to a millwright crew once and the seesaw was the thing that made it stick. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture two people on a seesaw and get them balanced in your head.

## Slide 21

Six steps. Step one, pick your reference point and then do not change it. The left end of the skid, a machined face, a bolt line -- it genuinely does not matter which, because the answer comes out as a distance from wherever you started. What kills people is measuring one component off the left end and the next off the centerline. Step two, break the load into pieces you know the weight of. Step three, measure to each piece's own balance point, not to its edge -- if the motor is three feet long, you want the middle of the motor. Step four, multiply each weight by its distance. Step five, add them up. Step six, divide by the total weight. What comes out is the distance from your reference point to the balance point of the whole load. And if you need it in two directions, you run the same six steps again along the other axis. On a compressor skid I helped plan, we ran it lengthwise and crosswise and found the balance point was nearly a foot off the centerline because of where the oil cooler sat. [PAUSE / ASK] Walk the six steps back in your head before you move on.

## Slide 22

A moment is weight times distance, and once that clicks the whole method becomes arithmetic. Think of a kid on a seesaw. A light kid far out balances a heavy kid sitting close in, because what matters is not weight alone and not distance alone, it is the two multiplied together. That product is the moment. Some folks will add weight and distance, and you can hear that it is wrong the moment you check the units -- pounds plus feet is not a thing. Someone might divide distance by weight, which flips the relationship and would tell you a heavier component has less influence, the opposite of what a seesaw does. Some people will divide by the component count, which is averaging, and averaging throws away exactly the information you are trying to keep. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture that seesaw before you answer. Make your call.

## Slide 23

Let us run one all the way through. Twenty foot skid, eight thousand pounds total. Six thousand pound component centered four feet from the left end, two thousand pound component centered sixteen feet from the left end. Reference point is the left end and it stays the left end. First moment: six thousand times four is twenty four thousand pound-feet. Second moment: two thousand times sixteen is thirty two thousand pound-feet. Notice something there -- the lighter component produced the bigger moment, because it is sitting so much further out. That is the seesaw doing its thing. Add them: fifty six thousand pound-feet. Total weight is eight thousand pounds. Divide and you get seven feet from the left end. Now the part I want you to do every single time: sanity check it. Seven feet is on the heavy side of the skid, which is where it belongs, because the heavy component is over there. If your number ever comes out on the light side, you have made a mistake and you know it before anybody else does. I have caught my own arithmetic errors this way more than once on a job trailer table. [PAUSE / ASK] Cover the numbers and work it yourself.

## Slide 24

Run the method. Pick the left end as your reference point. The heavy component gives you six thousand pounds times four feet, which is twenty four thousand. The light component gives you two thousand times sixteen, which is thirty two thousand. Add those together for fifty six thousand, then divide by the total weight of eight thousand pounds. That lands you at seven feet from the left end. Notice it sits much closer to the heavy component, which is what your gut should have told you before you did any math. Some folks will just name the heavy component's own position, forgetting that the lighter component still pulls the balance point toward itself. Someone might split the skid in half and call it the middle, which would only be right if the two components were equal. Some people will average the two positions and land between them without weighting, which ignores that one component is three times the other. [PAUSE / ASK] Do the arithmetic on paper, do not eyeball it. Make your call.

## Slide 25

Same method, three components, and one new wrinkle. Thirty foot skid. Pump at four thousand pounds, five feet in. Motor at three thousand pounds, twelve feet in. And the base frame itself at three thousand pounds. Now where does the frame count from? The frame is uniform along its length, so its own balance point is at its mid-length, fifteen feet. Multiply each one out: twenty thousand, thirty six thousand, forty five thousand pound-feet. Total is one hundred and one thousand. Total weight ten thousand pounds. Divide and you land at ten point one feet from the left end. Here is the part I want to hammer on. The frame. People forget the structure they are standing on. That base frame was thirty percent of the total weight, and if you leave it out your answer moves badly. On real equipment the skid or base frame commonly runs twenty to forty percent of the whole package. I have seen a calculation submitted with the pump and motor and no frame, and the number was off by more than two feet. [PAUSE / ASK] What structure would you have forgotten on that skid?

## Slide 26

The reference point is yours to pick. An end of the load, a machined face, a bolt line, the edge of the skid -- any of them works, because the method finds the balance point relative to wherever you started measuring. What will burn you is switching reference points partway through, measuring one component from the left end and the next from the centerline. Some folks will lock onto the left end as a rule, and the left end is a fine habit but it is a habit, not a requirement. Someone might insist on the geometric center, which is often an awkward place to measure from and has no special standing. Some people will anchor on the heaviest component, and that gives you a correct answer expressed from an odd starting line. [PAUSE / ASK] On a long vessel I worked, we measured everything off the north nozzle because it was the one thing everybody could see from anywhere on the pad. Make your call.

## Slide 27

This is the method that settles arguments. Set the load on two supports a known distance apart with a scale or load cell under each one, and read them. Add the two readings and you have the total weight. Then, to find how far the balance point sits from the left support, take the reading from the far support, multiply by the span, and divide by the total. Two conditions have to hold or the numbers lie to you. The load has to be level, and it has to be resting on those two supports and nothing else. If a corner of the skid is still touching a timber somewhere, that timber is carrying load your scales cannot see. The check that keeps you honest: the balance point always sits nearer the support that reads higher. And the real strength of this method is that it tells you about the load as it actually is today, right now, contents and all -- it does not care what the drawing says. I have used two load cells to settle a fifteen minute argument about a generator skid that everybody had a different number for. [PAUSE / ASK] Have you ever had load cells available on a job?

## Slide 28

Run the numbers. Thirty foot beam, scales at each end thirty feet apart. Left reads four thousand, right reads two thousand. Total weight is six thousand pounds, that part is just addition. For the position, take the far reading -- the right scale, two thousand pounds -- multiply by the thirty foot span to get sixty thousand, then divide by the six thousand pound total. Ten feet from the left end. And now the sanity check, which on this method is beautifully simple. The left scale is carrying twice what the right one is. So the balance point should be twice as close to the left as it is to the right. Ten feet to the left, twenty feet to the right. That is exactly a two to one ratio, so the answer is consistent. The most common mistake here is grabbing the near reading instead of the far one, which lands you on the wrong side of the beam. The ratio check catches it every time. I have watched a foreman catch that exact error in about four seconds using nothing but the ratio. [PAUSE / ASK] Which reading do you multiply by the span?

## Slide 29

Two scales, one reading at each end, and the balance point sits closer to the scale carrying more. Total weight is six thousand pounds. To find the distance from the left support, take the far scale reading, two thousand pounds, multiply by the span of thirty feet, and divide by the total. That is sixty thousand divided by six thousand, which is ten feet from the left end. Sanity check it -- the left scale carries twice what the right one does, so the balance point should be twice as close to the left, and ten feet versus twenty feet is exactly that. Some folks will land on the midspan out of habit, which would only be right if both scales read the same. Someone might use the near scale reading instead of the far one and end up on the wrong side entirely. Some people will guess low without checking that the two distances have to add up to the full span. [PAUSE / ASK] Always sanity check which side the answer should fall on. Make your call.

## Slide 30

This is why the method is worth the setup time. Add the two readings and you have the total weight. Compare the two readings against the span and you have the balance point. Two numbers out of one operation, and both of them are the numbers the lift plan is asking for. Some folks will say you only get weight, which throws away half of what the readings are telling you -- the difference between the two scales is exactly the information about where the balance point sits. Someone might think you only get the balance point, forgetting that the readings are pounds and pounds add. Some people will reach for sling tension, but that comes later, out of your hitch geometry and sling angles, not out of two scales under a load sitting on the ground. ## Slide 31

Section three. You have done the math, and now you are standing next to the load with slings on it. This section is about the last check before you commit, and it is the one that catches everything the paperwork could not know about. Contents nobody mentioned. A component that is not where the drawing put it. Water in the bottom of a vessel. The math is honest about the load you described. The trial lift is honest about the load in front of you. I have seen a calculation come out perfect and the load still hang crooked because there was forty gallons of coolant in a sump nobody drained. ## Slide 32

Here is the procedure and it is short. Everybody gets clear first -- and I mean before the load breaks contact with the ground, not as it comes up. Then you lift slow and you stop within a few inches. And then you do the part people skip: you actually stop and look. Does it come up level, or does one end hang low? If it tilts, you set it back down and you move your rigging. Not adjust it in the air. Not fight it with tag lines. Set it down. Then you try again, and you keep at it until the load comes up level. And if it will not come level after a couple of honest attempts, that is the load telling you your understanding of it is wrong, and the right move is to stop and go back to the plan. Here is the way I want you to think about this. At two inches off the ground, a bad hookup costs you twenty minutes. At ten feet, it costs a whole lot more than that. Same mistake, wildly different price, and the only variable is whether you checked. I watched a crew skip the trial lift on a tank once because they were behind schedule, and they spent the rest of the afternoon dealing with what happened next. [PAUSE / ASK] Does your crew actually stop and look, or just keep going?

## Slide 33

The trial lift did its job. It told you the hook is not over the balance point, and it told you while the load was still an inch off the ground where the consequence is nothing. You set it down, you move your rigging, you try again. Some folks will want to push on and fight it with tag lines, but tag lines are for controlling rotation and keeping hands off the load -- they are not for correcting a bad hookup, and asking them to do that overloads the people holding them. Someone might want to add a sling with the load in the air, which puts hands and bodies under a suspended load that has already shown you it is not behaving. Some people will suggest getting it clear and sorting it out up high, which takes the one situation where the load is still safe to correct and trades it for the one where it is not. [PAUSE / ASK] This is the cheapest information you will get all day. Make your call.

## Slide 34

The load will tell you what is wrong if you know how to read it. One end hangs low: the balance point is toward that low end, so move your hook that way. Load comes up level: you are over the balance point, go to work. Load rotates as it lifts rather than tilting front to back: your balance point is offset on the other axis, so set it down and check crosswise, because you solved one direction and not the other. Load rolls in the slings: your pick points are below the balance point, and that is not a hook position problem, that is a re-rig. Now the one I want burned in. The low end is the heavy end. Low equals heavy. The most common error I see in the field is somebody watching a load hang low on the left, deciding the right side must need help, and moving the hook right, which makes it worse. Low end, heavy end, move toward it. Say it a couple of times. I have had to walk a young rigger back from exactly that mistake on a live pick. [PAUSE / ASK] Which end is the heavy end?

## Slide 35

The load is telling you where the weight is, and you read it exactly the way you would read a scale. A suspended load rotates until its balance point ends up beneath the hook, so whichever end swings down is the end the balance point is closer to. Low on the right means heavy on the right, which means you hooked up too far left. Some folks will say it is already under the hook, but if it were, the load would be hanging level -- the tilt is the evidence that it is not. Someone might read it backward and call the high end the heavy end, which is the single most common misread on a trial lift and it sends you moving the rigging the wrong direction. Some people will fall back on the geometric center, but the load just demonstrated it does not agree with that assumption. [PAUSE / ASK] Watch which way it dips and move the hook that way. Make your call.

## Slide 36

Here is the rule, and I want you to lock it in the way your earlier training framed it, because you will be tested on it exactly this way. When a load on a fixed-attachment bridle hangs low on one end, that low end is the heavy end, and the first correction is to shorten the sling on that low, heavy side. Shortening the low side lifts the heavy end up until the load comes level. Say that back to yourself, because the trap is to do the opposite -- lengthening the low side just lets the heavy end drop further, and it is the single most common wrong move. Same result the other direction: you can lengthen the high, light side instead. Your second option is to move the attachment toward the heavy end so the hook lines up over the balance point. Your third, when the attachment points are fixed where they are, is to change effective leg length with turnbuckles or chain lever hoists. Now the things that are not fixes. Do not hold a load level with tag lines -- that is not what tag lines are for, and it puts real force on the people holding them. Do not add counterweight, because now you are picking something you never calculated. Do not adjust rigging with the load in the air, ever. And hoist speed does nothing for balance. One more thing people miss: the moment your legs are different lengths, your angles change, and different angles mean different tension in each leg. So go back and check your sling capacities against the geometry you actually ended up with. On a steel job I watched a crew shorten one leg with a come-along and never revisit the tension math. ## Slide 37

You have two honest options. Move where you are picking from so the hook ends up over the balance point, or change the effective length of the legs so the load hangs level from where you already are. Turnbuckles and chain lever hoists in the legs are the standard way to do the second one. Some folks will talk about hoist speed, but speed has nothing to do with balance -- moving faster just means the swing carries more energy. Someone might say tighten the high side, which is backward: shortening the high leg lifts that end further and makes the tilt worse. Some people will suggest adding weight to bring the light end down, and now you are lifting a load you did not plan for and did not calculate. ## Slide 39

A partly full container does not have one balance point. It has a range, and the load picks where in that range it wants to be based on what you just did with it. Tilt it and the liquid runs downhill and takes the balance point with it. Start or stop suddenly and the contents surge. And here is the part that makes it genuinely dangerous: it feeds itself. A small tilt moves the liquid, the liquid moving increases the tilt, and that increases the flow. It can get away from you. This is not just liquids either. Loose bulk material in a bin, unsecured tooling in a gang box, a stack of parts that can slide -- same behavior. So what do you do? First choice, take the variable away: empty it or fill it. A full container has nowhere for the contents to go and behaves like a solid. If you genuinely have to lift it partly full, then you slow everything down, you avoid abrupt starts and stops, you use baffles or restraint if the vessel has them, and you plan around the balance point being a zone instead of a point. I have seen a half-full chemical tote get lively on a crew who thought it was a routine pick. [PAUSE / ASK] What is the first question to ask about a closed container?

## Slide 40

A partly full tank does not have one balance point, it has a moving one. Tip the tank, swing it, stop it quickly, and the liquid runs to one side and takes the balance point with it. Some folks will say the real issue is the extra weight, and unaccounted weight is absolutely a problem, but weight you did not know about is a problem you can solve by weighing -- a shifting balance point is a problem that shows up mid-lift. Someone might point at slinging difficulty, which is a nuisance, not the hazard. Some people will reach for wind load, but the liquid is inside the tank and does not change what the wind sees. [PAUSE / ASK] The fix is usually to fill it or empty it, not to lift it partly full. Make your call.

## Slide 41

Tall and narrow with the weight up high. In the air, hanging from above, this load is fine -- it is a pendulum and it behaves. The danger comes at set-down, and specifically in the seconds when the load transitions from hanging on your hook to standing on its own base. That is when the high balance point becomes a liability. All it takes is ground that is not level, or one timber that settles more than the others, or a soft spot in the surface, and the load starts to lean. And once it leans past a certain point, its own weight finishes the job. There is no catching it. So the controls are all about the set-down. Prepare that area before the load arrives -- level it, know what the bearing surface will do, get adequate blocking under it with enough area to spread the load. Keep your tag lines on until the load is genuinely stable, not until it touches down. And if it is tall enough to need bracing or guying, get that on before anybody releases the rigging. ## Slide 43

Sometimes you do not have the information and the work still has to happen. The federal rules address this head on for assembly and disassembly work, in paragraph 1404(h)(6) of Subpart CC, and the thinking carries straight across to rigging. The rule is in two parts. Where your method of keeping the load stable depends on knowing the balance point, you have to identify it. And where you do not have enough information to identify it accurately, you are required to use measures designed to keep an inaccurate assumption from producing dangerous movement. Read that second part carefully, because it is not permission to guess and carry on. It is a requirement to actively control for being wrong. In practice that looks like a slow deliberate trial lift with everybody clear. Tag lines on before it comes off the ground, not after. A bigger exclusion zone than you would normally set. Restraint against rotation. A conservative assumption with margin built in rather than an optimistic one. And a clear, agreed stop point -- everybody knows in advance what behavior means we set it down and start over. Under 29 CFR 1926.1419(j) anybody on that crew can call that stop, and on a load you are unsure about, that authority matters more than usual. ## Slide 49

A suspended load always ends up with its balance point under the hook. That is not a tendency, it is the outcome, and it happens the instant the load goes free of the ground. ## Slide 50
