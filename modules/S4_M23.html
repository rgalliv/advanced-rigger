Section 1

What BTH-1 Is and Why It Exists

What BTH-1 Covers

ASME BTH-1 -- 'Design of Below-the-Hook Lifting Devices' -- is the design standard for devices that hang below the crane hook: spreader beams, lifting beams, C-hooks, coil grabs, and more.

It is written for the engineer who designs the device -- not a wage sheet, not a paint code.

BTH-1 and B30.20 Work Together

Two standards cover a below-the-hook device across its life:

  • BTH-1 -- the design (structural and mechanical engineering)
  • B30.20 -- marking, construction, inspection, testing, and operation

The engineer designs to BTH-1; the device is then marked and run under B30.20. Partners, not duplicates.

Knowledge Check

BTH-1 and ASME B30.20 divide the work this way:

An Engineered Product, Not Shop Rigging

A below-the-hook device is an engineered product -- designed to a standard, rated for a specific load, and tested.

It is not something you improvise, weld together, or modify. Its capacity is only valid because it was built and tested that way.

Section 2

Design Categories A and B

What Sets the Design Category

A device's Design Category is set by how predictable and well-defined its loads and conditions are -- how sure the engineer can be about what it will actually see in service.

Known, controlled loads point one way; uncertain or harsh service points another.

Knowledge Check

In BTH-1, the Design Category of a device is set by:

Category A: Factor of 2

Design Category A -- loads and conditions well-defined and predictable:

Design factor = 2 (against yield strength)

The leaner factor, allowed because the service is well understood.

Category B: Factor of 3

Design Category B -- loads or conditions less predictable or more severe:

Design factor = 3 (against yield strength)

The larger, more conservative factor. More uncertainty demands more margin.

What the Design Factor Means

The design factor is the margin between working stress and the material's yield -- the point where metal permanently deforms.

Factor of 2 = about twice that margin; factor of 3 = more still. It is the engineered cushion against overload, surprises, and wear -- not a count, a speed, or a price.

Section 3

Service Classes 0-4

Service Class Is Fatigue Life

A device's Service Class describes its fatigue life -- the number of load cycles it is designed to withstand.

Every pick-and-set is a cycle. Metal cycled enough times can crack from fatigue even below its rated load.

Knowledge Check

In BTH-1, the Service Class of a device describes its:

The Service Class Scale

BTH-1 defines Service Classes 0 through 4 by load cycles:

  • Class 0 -- up to about 20,000 cycles (lowest duty)
  • Classes 1, 2, 3 -- progressively more cycles
  • Class 4 -- over 2,000,000 cycles (highest duty)

Higher class number = more cycles = more fatigue-resistant design.

Knowledge Check

The lowest service class, Service Class 0, is designed for:

Reading the Class Direction

Moving from Class 0 up toward Class 4:

  • More load cycles, greater fatigue life
  • Does not change how heavy a single pick can be -- that is the rated load

Class answers 'how many times'; rated load answers 'how heavy'.

Knowledge Check

Moving from Service Class 0 up toward Service Class 4 means the device is designed for:

Undersized Class = Fatigue Cracks

Put a low-service-class device on a high-cycle job and it can fatigue-crack well before the rated load is ever exceeded.

Fatigue adds up cycle by cycle -- it does not care that each pick is under capacity. Match the class to the job's duty cycle.

Choosing a below-the-hook device means matching both:

Rated load alone is not enough. Category and class complete the picture.

Section 4

Marking, Operation, and the Rigger's Duties

Required Markings

Under B30.20, an engineered device is marked with its rated load plus:

  • Manufacturer name and contact, serial number
  • Device weight (if over 100 lb)
  • BTH-1 Design Category and BTH-1 Service Class

Knowledge Check

Under B30.20, an engineered below-the-hook device must be marked with its rated load plus:

Rated Load Is a Hard Limit

The rated load is the maximum the device is engineered to lift -- a hard limit you never exceed.

Exceeding it eats into the design factor invisibly. If the load is heavier, you get a bigger device -- never a hopeful pick.

Knowledge Check

The rated load marked on a below-the-hook device is:

Count the Combined Weight

Against the crane's capacity chart, count the combined weight:

Load + below-the-hook device + rigging

A heavy spreader or lifting beam plus slings adds up fast -- the crane feels every pound. Do not check the bare load alone.

Knowledge Check

When checking the crane's capacity for a lift using a below-the-hook device, you must count:

Inspect Each Shift

A below-the-hook device is inspected each shift for:

  • Structural deformation, cracks, and excessive wear
  • Loose or missing guards, fasteners, nameplates
  • Legible markings and working controls

Anything that looks like a hazard = out of service until a qualified person clears it.

Knowledge Check

A below-the-hook device should be inspected:

Damaged or Unmarked: Take It Out of Service

Damaged device, or missing/unreadable markings (rated load, category, class) = out of service until a qualified person evaluates it.

Do not lift with what you cannot verify. Do not repaint a rating you cannot confirm. Do not field-weld a fix.

A Realistic Find

You find a spreader beam with a clear rated load but no readable Design Category or Service Class.

Hold it out of service until it is verified. The missing category and class are required markings -- without them you cannot confirm it suits your loads and duty cycle.
