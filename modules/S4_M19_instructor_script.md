## Slide 15

The latch on a hook does one job, and it is not the job people sometimes give it. A safety latch, or a mousing of wire when there is no latch, bridges the throat opening so the sling or the load cannot jump out if the rigging goes slack or shifts. That is retention, and it matters. But the latch is not a load-bearing part -- it is not built to carry weight, and it will fail if you make it. So you never rig a load so that it bears on the latch. The hook body carries the load; the latch just keeps things from falling out of the throat. Keep the two jobs separate in your head. ## Slide 22

Eyebolts come in two kinds, and mixing them up bends and snaps hardware. A non-shoulder or plain eyebolt has no collar at its base, so it is built for vertical, in-line pull only -- straight up its axis. The one-line rule: plain means vertical only, shoulder for angles. And even a shoulder eyebolt at an angle loses a lot of capacity, which is why hoist rings exist. Back to the side-load chart, because this is exactly the kind of angle that gets missed. Seventy degrees off in-line falls in the forty-six to ninety degree band, and that band cuts the rated capacity in half -- about a fifty percent reduction. ## Slide 47

This is the eyebolt trap, and it is a common one. A plain, non-shoulder eyebolt is designed for vertical, in-line pull only -- it has no shoulder to brace a side force -- so you do not use it for the angled leg at all. Loaded at an angle it will bend or break. The right move is a shoulder eyebolt seated flush with the eye in the plane of the pull, or better yet a swivel hoist ring that holds full rating at the angle. Some folks think an eyebolt handles any direction, but that is the plain one's downfall. Someone might grease the threads to let it swivel, but an eyebolt does not swivel and must be tight. Some people will load it at the angle at half rating, but a plain eyebolt at an angle is not a half-rating situation, it is a wrong-tool situation. Plain eyebolt equals vertical only. ## Slide 50

A pin that will not seat flush is a shackle telling you something is wrong, and it is a removal, not a workaround. If the pin threads in but its shoulder will not draw down flush against the body, the pin is not fully engaged -- the threads may be damaged, the pin may be the wrong one, or the body may be distorted -- and the shackle comes out of service until it is sorted out. Some folks will use it if the pin is most of the way in, but most of the way is not engaged. Some people will hammer the pin to force it, which damages it further. A shackle pin seats flush and turns by hand to snug; anything else is out.
