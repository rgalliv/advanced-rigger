Section 1

Blocks and Sheaves

Sheave vs Block

Two words people mix up:

  • Sheave -- the grooved wheel (pulley) the rope rides in
  • Block -- the housing (shell / cheeks) that holds one or more sheaves on a pin, with a hook or shackle and often a becket

A block contains sheaves. Parts: shell/cheeks, sheave(s), pin, bearing, attachment (hook/shackle), becket.

The Becket

The becket is the built-in anchor point on a block for dead-ending a rope -- the fixed point where the rope end attaches so the rest can run through the sheaves.

When you reeve several parts of line, the dead end lands on the becket.

The sheave groove must match the rope size so it supports the rope's round shape without harming it:

  • Too tight -- pinches and crushes the rope
  • Too wide -- lets the rope flatten and lose shape
  • Worn / corrugated -- a channel worn by an old rope chews up the next one

Sheave D/d and Rope Life

A running sheave bends the rope every pass, so its D/d ratio (sheave diameter to rope diameter) must be large -- much larger than a sling bent once over a pin.

A small sheave bends the rope sharply thousands of times. That repeated flexing fatigues the wires and shortens rope life dramatically.

Follow the wire rope manufacturer's minimum sheave D/d; crane load sheaves are sized large on purpose. Anchored to ASME B30.5.

Section 2

Reeving and Mechanical Advantage

Parts of Line

The parts of line are the separate rope segments running between the fixed block and the traveling load block that support the load.

Count the supporting segments -- not the sheaves, not the feet of rope. Four segments holding the load block = four parts of line.

Knowledge Check

The 'parts of line' in a block-and-tackle are:

Mechanical Advantage

The theoretical mechanical advantage equals the number of parts of line supporting the load.

Line pull = Load / Parts of line (before friction)

Four parts = 4:1 advantage; your hauling pull is about one quarter of the load.

Knowledge Check

Friction Is the Tax

The actual advantage is always less than theoretical because each sheave has friction (bearing drag and rope flexing).

More parts multiply capacity but add friction and reduce speed. Past a point, adding parts barely helps -- friction eats the gain.

Worked Example: Line Pull

A load block with 5 parts of line supports a 5,000 lb load.

Line pull = 5,000 / 5 = 1,000 lb (before friction)

The 5 parts share the load; the hauling line feels about one fifth. Add friction and the real pull is somewhat higher.

Section 3

Fleet Angle and Fairleads

What Fleet Angle Is

The fleet angle is the angle between the rope and a line square (perpendicular) to the sheave or drum, measured at the rope's extreme lead.

Picture the rope wanting to come straight in, square to the sheave. The fleet angle is how far off square it actually runs.

Knowledge Check

The fleet angle is the angle between the rope and:

Keep the Fleet Angle Small

Keep the fleet angle small:

  • About 1.5 degrees to a sheave or smooth drum
  • About 2 degrees to a grooved drum

Too large a fleet angle makes the rope climb the flange, rub, and wear -- and pile up badly on a drum.

Knowledge Check

Two-blocking is the condition where:

Fairleads

A fairlead -- rollers or sheaves -- guides a rope onto a drum or around a corner at an acceptable angle, keeping the fleet angle in check and preventing chafing.

When the geometry would give a bad lead, add a fairlead to lead the rope fair.

Knowledge Check

Two-blocking is treated as a severe hazard mainly because:

When Fleet Angle Goes Wrong

If the fleet angle grows too large:

  • The rope climbs the sheave flange instead of seating in the groove
  • It rubs and wears both the rope and the flange
  • On a drum it piles up, crosses, and crushes wraps

A wide fleet angle can even throw the rope out of the groove.

Section 4

Snatch Blocks, Resultant Load, and Inspection

The Snatch Block

A snatch block has a side plate (cheek) that swings open, so you can drop a bight (loop) of line into it without threading the rope end.

That lets you add it anywhere along a line to redirect the pull -- change the direction the rope runs -- without re-reeving.

Knowledge Check

Before a snatch block is anchored to a beam or padeye, someone must confirm:

The Resultant Load

A change-of-direction block feels the resultant -- the vector sum of the two line tensions coming into the sheave -- not just the line tension.

The sharper the turn, the larger the resultant. The block and its anchor must carry that combined force.

Knowledge Check

The load a change-of-direction (snatch) block and its anchor actually feel is:

Rate the Block and the Anchor

Because a snatch block can see up to twice the line tension, rate both the block and its anchor for the resultant -- not the line pull.

The anchor (padeye, beam, deadman) must hold the full combined force. Sizing it to the line pull alone can let it fail on a sharp redirect.

Sheave Inspection and Removal

Remove a sheave from service for:

  • Worn, corrugated, or cracked grooves, or grooves worn out of round
  • Cracked or chipped flanges
  • A sheave that will not turn freely -- a failing bearing or pin

Spin each sheave by hand. A dragging sheave grinds rope instead of rolling it.

Block Inspection and Removal

Remove the block from service for:

  • Cracked, bent, or distorted cheeks or shell
  • A worn or bent sheave pin
  • A damaged, worn, or opened hook or shackle
  • Missing or broken keepers/guards; missing or illegible rating

90 Degrees: About 1.4 Times

Filling in the resultant curve: a 90-degree change of direction gives a resultant of about 1.4 times the line tension (the square root of two).

A right-angle redirect already puts about 40 percent more than the line pull into the block and anchor -- climbing to 2x at a full reversal.
