Section 1

The Load Angle Factor, Derived

From a Number to an Understanding

In the last module the Load Angle Factor (LAF) was a value you looked up or were given. Here we build it from scratch so you can reconstruct it on any job, with any geometry, without a table.

The chain of reasoning for every angled pick:

  1. Find the share of load on the leg (weight and geometry)
  2. Find the Load Angle Factor from the leg's angle
  3. Multiply: leg tension = LAF x share of load

Sharing the load was M02. The Load Angle Factor is this module. They are two separate multipliers and both must be applied.

The Sling-to-Horizontal Angle

The angle that sets tension is the sling-to-horizontal angle: the angle between the sling leg and the horizontal plane of the load.

  • Leg straight up = 90 degrees (no lean, least tension)
  • Leg leaning out to a wider pick point = smaller angle (more tension)

Do not measure from vertical. The angle from vertical is the complement -- it reads 30 when the true sling-to-horizontal is 60, and that swap will size your slings wrong.

Knowledge Check

The sling angle that drives leg tension is measured between the sling leg and:

LAF = Leg Length / Height

The house formula, and the one printed in the rigger reference booklet:

Load Angle Factor = Leg Length (L) / Vertical Height (H)

  • L = the slant length of the sling leg, pick point to hook
  • H = the vertical distance from the load up to the hook (headroom)

Example: a 20 ft leg with 15 ft of headroom gives LAF = 20 / 15 = 1.33. Each leg feels 1.33 times its share.

The factor is always 1 or greater. A factor below 1 means you measured something backwards.

Why L/H Equals 1/sin(angle)

The tape-measure shortcut and the trigonometry are the same thing.

In the rigging triangle, the sine of the sling-to-horizontal angle is:

sin(angle) = H / L (opposite over hypotenuse)

So its inverse is:

1 / sin(angle) = L / H = Load Angle Factor

This is why a shallow angle explodes tension: as the angle drops, its sine drops, and 1 divided by a small number gets large fast.

Worked Example: Two Multipliers

A 12,000 lb load on a symmetric 3-leg bridle, each leg at 20 ft length with 15 ft of headroom.

  1. Share of load: 12,000 / 3 legs = 4,000 lb per leg
  2. Load Angle Factor: 20 / 15 = 1.33
  3. Leg tension: 1.33 x 4,000 = 5,320 lb per leg

Size the sling for 5,320 lb (times any dynamic factor), not the 4,000 lb share. The angle added over 1,300 lb per leg.

The 30-Degree Floor

Most standards, load charts, and employers set 30 degrees to horizontal as a working minimum for sling angles.

The reason is the curve: below 30 degrees you pay a steep tension penalty for a small gain in reach or headroom. You overload slings, hardware, and pick points to save a little vertical room -- a bad trade.

When the geometry pushes you below 30, the fix is to change the geometry -- move the pick points in, add headroom, or use a spreader bar -- not to accept the tension.

Sling-to-Horizontal vs Included Angle

The Included Angle

The included angle is the angle between the two sling legs, measured at the hook where they meet.

  • Wide, flat V = large included angle
  • Legs nearly parallel and vertical = small included angle

This is a different measurement from the sling-to-horizontal angle. Confusing the two is the most common angle error in rigging.

Load charts and standards quote the sling-to-horizontal angle -- leg to the level. Always.

  • Actual factor (30 to horizontal): 2.00
  • Result: nearly double the tension, chart looking honored

Section 3

The D/d Ratio and Bearing Efficiency

What the D/d Ratio Is

The D/d ratio measures how sharply a sling bends where it wraps something.

  • D = diameter of the object the sling body bends around (pin, shackle bow, load corner, hook saddle)
  • d = diameter of the sling's own body (the rope)

A large D/d is a gentle bend. A small D/d is a tight bend, and a tight bend costs capacity -- the outer fibers of the rope are stretched hard while the inner ones go slack.

Protecting the Bend

Sharp corners and small-diameter contact do two kinds of damage: they cut the sling, and they tighten the D/d ratio and steal capacity.

The fix is the same for both:

  • Corner protectors and softeners -- put a larger radius between sling and edge
  • Saddles and blocks -- widen the effective D at the contact point

The cheap piece of gear protects the expensive one. Keep softeners on the truck.

The Basket Hitch in Detail

A basket cradles the load, giving two supporting parts of sling. With both legs truly vertical, capacity is up to 200 percent of the vertical rating.

The moment the legs spread to reach around the load, they take on a sling angle -- and the Load Angle Factor from Section 1 cuts the basket capacity right back down.

A wide basket is not a 200 percent basket. Apply the angle factor to the spread legs exactly as you would for a bridle.

Basket Worked Example

A wire rope sling rated 10,000 lb vertical, used in a basket:

  • Legs vertical: up to 2 x 10,000 = 20,000 lb
  • Legs at 45 degrees to horizontal: the spread applies a factor; the effective capacity drops well below 20,000 lb

Never rate a spread basket at the full doubled number. The angle is always working against you once the legs lean.

Endless Roundslings

An endless roundsling is a continuous loop of load-bearing yarns inside a woven protective cover.

  • The core yarns carry the load; the cover takes abrasion and carries nothing
  • Conforms around a load, rotates to spread wear, grips gently on finished surfaces
  • Works in vertical, choker, and basket hitches

The load-bearing core is hidden. A cut or damaged cover means you cannot verify the core -- remove it from service.

Metal Mesh and Matching the Type

Metal mesh slings -- woven wire and cross rods -- resist heat, sharp edges, and abrasion that would destroy a synthetic.

Match the sling to the hazard:

Load / hazardReach for
Heat, sharp edges, abrasionmetal mesh

General-purpose, ruggedwire rope

The metal fabric can mark a fine finish -- do not reach for it on delicate surfaces.

The Pre-Use Discipline

No matter the sling type, the lift-day check never changes:

  1. Confirm a legible identification tag
  2. Read the rated capacity for the exact hitch and angle you will use
  3. Inspect the body for damage per the sling type

No readable tag = out of service. A sling you cannot identify is a sling you cannot trust, no matter how good it looks.
