Section 1

Why Legs Do Not Share Evenly

The Rigid Load Problem

A rigid load cannot flex to settle onto all its legs. Like a stiff table on an uneven floor, it rocks onto two legs while the others only touch.

  • Flexible load: sags and conforms, sharing reasonably across legs
  • Rigid load: two legs can carry nearly the whole weight; the other two only steady it against tipping
  • You cannot know from looking which two legs are the working pair
  • The load hangs perfectly stable while badly overloading two legs

This is why rigging practice sizes a 4-leg bridle on a rigid load as though only two legs carry the load.

Angle Is the Other Half of the Story

Leg count is not the only thing that decides leg load. Angle does too -- and it matters more than most riggers expect.

  • A vertical leg carries only its share of the weight
  • A leaning leg carries its share PLUS an angle penalty
  • The flatter the leg, the larger the penalty -- rising steeply as the sling approaches horizontal
  • The load never changed weight; the geometry multiplied the tension

The rest of this module is about putting a number on that penalty and using it to size slings correctly.

The Load Angle Factor Method

Step 1: The Load Angle Factor

The Load Angle Factor (LAF) is the multiplier that captures the angle penalty.

Load Angle Factor = Leg Length ÷ Headroom
LAF = L ÷ H
  • Leg length (L): the actual length of the sling leg, hook to load
  • Headroom (H): the vertical height from the load attachment up to the hook
  • A vertical leg: L and H are equal, so LAF = 1.0 (no penalty)
  • Any leaning leg: L is longer than H, so LAF is greater than 1.0

LAF is always 1.0 or greater. If you ever calculate a factor below 1.0, you divided upside down.

Step 2: Share of Load

Before applying the angle penalty, find each leg's fair share of the weight.

Share of Load = Total Weight ÷ Number of Legs
SOL = W ÷ (legs)
  • On a symmetric load, each leg's share is simply the total divided by the leg count you are designing for
  • Remember: on a rigid 4-leg bridle, that count is 2, not 4
  • This is the starting weight, before the angle penalty is applied

Step 3: Sling Tension

Multiply the share by the angle factor to get the tension the leg actually feels.

Sling Tension = Share of Load × Load Angle Factor
T = SOL × LAF
  • The tension -- not the share -- is what you size the sling against
  • The gap between tension and share is the cost of the angle
  • Then apply the sling's rated capacity and design factor to the tension

Size the sling to the tension. Sizing to the share is the error that leaves a leg undersized the moment it leans.

Worked Example: Symmetric Bridle

Given: a 12,000 lb symmetric load on a 3-leg bridle.
Leg length L = 20 ft; headroom H = 15 ft.

Step 1 -- Load Angle Factor
LAF = L ÷ H = 20 ÷ 15 = 1.33

Step 2 -- Share of Load
SOL = 12,000 ÷ 3 = 4,000 lb

Step 3 -- Sling Tension
T = SOL × LAF = 4,000 × 1.33 = 5,320 lb per leg

Each leg feels 5,320 lb, not the 4,000 lb share. Size the sling to 5,320 lb (then apply the design factor).

Section 3

Off-Level Pick Points

When the Hook Is Off the CG

When the pick point is not over the center of gravity, the two lift points carry unequal shares.

  • The point nearer the CG carries the greater share
  • The point farther from the CG carries the smaller share
  • The load hangs off level until corrected -- and loads the near leg hard while it does
  • You must split the load between the points before sizing either sling

Think of two people carrying a plank with a heavy box near one end. The person nearer the box strains; the far person barely works.

Splitting the Load: The Moment Method Again

Use the moment method to split an off-level load between two points -- the same tool that located the CG.

Share at A = Weight × (distance CG-to-B) ÷ Span
Share at B = Weight × (distance CG-to-A) ÷ Span
  • Each point's share uses the distance to the opposite point
  • The two shares always add up to the total weight -- a built-in check
  • The nearer point gets the bigger share, exactly as the plank predicts

Worked Example: Off-Level Two-Point Pick

Given: 8,200 lb load between two points.
CG is 4 ft from point 1 and 9 ft from point 2. Span = 13 ft.

Share at point 1 (nearer) = 8,200 × 9 ÷ 13 = 5,677 lb
Share at point 2 (farther) = 8,200 × 4 ÷ 13 = 2,523 lb

Check: 5,677 + 2,523 = 8,200 lb  ✓

Then apply each leg's angle factor to its own share to get leg tension.

Point 1 is nearer the CG and carries more than twice what point 2 carries. Size its sling accordingly -- and add its angle penalty.

Angle Limits and Corrections

Minimum Sling Angle

Because tension climbs steeply as slings flatten, rigging practice sets a practical floor on sling angle.

Angle from horizontalRoughly what the leg carries
90 degrees (vertical)Its share -- no penalty
60 degreesAbout 1.15 times its share
45 degreesAbout 1.4 times its share
30 degrees (practical floor)About 2 times its share
Below 30 degreesTension climbs sharply -- do not go here without engineering

30 degrees from horizontal is the widely taught minimum. Below it, get an engineer or change the rigging.

The Governing Leg on Unequal Bridles

When bridle legs differ in length, they hang at different angles and carry different tensions.

  • The flattest leg (smallest angle to horizontal) carries the highest tension
  • Base the pick's capacity on that worst-case leg
  • Do not average the legs -- the easy legs do not cover for the flat one
  • When legs are unequal, the smallest H/R (steepest-to-flattest) ratio governs

If the flattest leg is safe, the whole pick is safe. Find it first.

Correcting an Off-Level Pick

To restore even leg sharing, level the load. Two legitimate methods (from the CG module):

  1. Adjust effective leg length with turnbuckles or chain lever hoists to level the load from fixed attachment points
  2. Move the pick points so the hook ends up over the CG

Not acceptable: increasing hoist speed; adding a leg to an unbalanced arrangement; holding it level with tag lines; accepting the tilt and lifting anyway.

After adjusting leg lengths, recheck: new lengths mean new angles, which mean new tensions. Re-verify sling capacity.

Putting It Together: The Rigger's Sequence

For any bridle pick, the full sequence is:

  1. Get the load weight and locate the CG (Module 1)
  2. Decide the design leg count -- 2 for a rigid 4-leg bridle
  3. If the hook is off the CG, split the load between points (moment method)
  4. Find each leg's angle factor (L / H)
  5. Multiply each leg's share by its angle factor to get tension
  6. Identify the governing (flattest) leg
  7. Size each sling to its tension, apply the design factor, verify the angle is above the 30-degree floor
