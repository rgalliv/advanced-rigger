## Slide 6

To put a number on friction, we use the coefficient of friction. It is a plain number, written as the Greek letter mu, that describes how much a particular pair of surfaces grip each other -- steel on steel, steel on wood, rubber on concrete, each has its own value. The higher the number, the more resistance, and the more force it takes to slide the load. A greased steel surface has a low coefficient; a rough dry one is high. It is separate from the weight -- the coefficient just decides how much of that weight comes back at you as friction. ## Slide 8

Here is something you feel every time you move a load by hand. Static friction, the breakaway friction that holds a still load in place, is higher than kinetic friction, the sliding friction once the load is moving. So you push harder and harder, the load suddenly breaks loose, and then it slides easier than you expected. That sudden drop is why a load can lunge and get away from you the instant it starts -- the force you needed to break it free is now more than it takes to keep it going. So you plan for the breakaway and you are ready when it lets go. On a job I have watched a load break loose all at once and jump forward because static friction gave way.
