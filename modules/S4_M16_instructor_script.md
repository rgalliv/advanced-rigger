## Slide 1

Welcome to Unequal Leg Loading and Off-Level Pick Points, module two of the Advanced Rigger stage. Last module you learned to find where a load balances. This module answers the question that comes right after: now that you know where it balances, how much load is actually in each sling leg? And the answer surprises people, because it is almost never what you would guess by dividing the weight by the number of legs. A four-leg bridle does not divide by four. A sling that leans carries more than its share. And a pick point that is not over the balance point loads one leg harder than the others. ## Slide 4

Start with why this happens, because once you see it you will never divide by four again. A rigid load cannot bend. So when it hangs from four legs, it does exactly what a stiff table does on an uneven floor -- it settles onto two legs and the other two barely carry anything. They are touching, they are steadying the load against tipping, but they are not sharing the weight. A flexible load is different. Something that can sag will conform and spread itself across the legs more evenly. But most of what you pick on a four-leg bridle -- machine bases, weldments, structural pieces -- is rigid. And here is the part that gets people: the load hangs perfectly level and stable while two of its legs are badly overloaded. Nothing looks wrong. You cannot even tell by looking which two legs drew the short straw. So the practice is simple and conservative: on a rigid load, size the bridle as if two legs carry the whole weight. I have watched a rigid machine base come up on a four-leg bridle and rock onto two corners in the first inch, and the crew who sized every leg for half the load slept fine. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture a stiff table on a floor that is not flat, and which legs take the weight.

## Slide 5

This is the single most important idea in the whole module, so we lead with it. On a rigid load, a four-leg bridle does not split the weight four ways. Because the load will not flex, it is entirely possible for two legs to end up carrying almost the whole thing while the other two are just there keeping it from tipping. Think about setting a rigid table on an uneven floor -- it rocks on two legs and the other two barely touch. Same physics hanging from a hook. Some folks will divide by four and feel good about it, and that is exactly the assumption that overloads a sling, because the real share on the working legs is far more than a quarter. Someone might say all four share equally, which is only true if the load is perfectly symmetric, the legs are perfectly matched, and the hook is dead over the balance point -- three things that are rarely all true at once. Some people will guess the longest legs carry most, but on a rigid load it is the geometry and the fit-up, not leg length alone, that decides. ## Slide 6

So if we cannot count on four legs, what do we design for? Two. The standard rigging practice on a rigid load is to calculate as though only two of the four legs carry the load, and let the other two be a stability bonus you do not rely on. That is conservative on purpose, because being wrong in this direction means a leg has margin it did not need, and being wrong the other direction means a leg tears. Some folks will divide by four, which is the trap we just talked about. Someone might split the difference and divide by three, which feels like a reasonable compromise and is not grounded in anything -- the load does not care about your compromise. Some people will treat it as a single vertical, which ignores the geometry entirely. Design for two legs carrying, and you are rigging the way the load actually behaves. [PAUSE / ASK] On a job where the drawing called a four-leg pick, we still sized every leg for half the load. Make your call.

## Slide 7

Now the second force at work, and it is the one that turns this from a counting problem into a calculation. It is not just how many legs -- it is the angle each leg hangs at. A leg that runs straight up and down carries just its portion of the weight, nothing extra. But the moment a leg leans, it carries its portion plus a penalty, and that penalty grows fast as the leg gets flatter. Think about why. A leaning leg is not just holding the load up, it is also pulling inward against the other legs, and that inward pull is extra tension the vertical leg never had. Flatten the leg toward horizontal and that inward pull dominates, and the tension can run to double, triple, more than the share. The load never got heavier. The geometry did the damage. Everything left in this module is about measuring that penalty and respecting it. I have seen a rigger hold a leg out flat to clear a beam and never feel what that did to the tension. [PAUSE / ASK] Think about holding a weight with your arm straight down versus straight out to the side, and which burns.

## Slide 8

This is the relationship that governs everything downstream, so let us be very clear about the direction. As the sling gets flatter -- as the angle from the horizontal gets smaller -- the tension in the leg goes up, and it goes up fast. A leg at a low, flat angle is fighting to hold a load that wants to pull it straight sideways, and that fight shows up as tension that can run to several times the actual weight it is lifting. Some folks will say tension drops as the sling flattens, which is backward and it is the misread that fails slings. Someone might think the angle does not matter, that a leg carries what it carries, but the whole reason we do this math is that it changes enormously with angle. Some people will imagine it goes to zero, which is exactly wrong -- flat is where tension is highest, not lowest. [PAUSE / ASK] Picture pulling a clothesline tight and dead level, and how hard that gets. Make your call.

## Slide 9

## Slide 10

Step one, the load angle factor. It is leg length divided by headroom, and those are two measurements you can take with a tape. Leg length is the sling leg itself, hook down to the load. Headroom is the straight-up vertical distance from where the sling attaches on the load up to the hook. Divide leg length by headroom. On a vertical leg those two are the same number, so you get one, no penalty. On any leaning leg, the sling leg is longer than the straight-up height -- it has to be, it is taking the long diagonal path -- so the factor comes out bigger than one. That factor is always one or more. If you ever get a number under one, you flipped the division, put headroom over leg length, and you need to turn it right side up. Keep leg length on top. On a compressor skid I helped plan, we measured leg length and headroom right there on the pad and had the factor before the crane even swung over. [PAUSE / ASK] Which measurement is the long diagonal, and which is straight up?

## Slide 12

The vertical leg is your reference point and its factor is exactly one. When a leg runs straight up and down, the leg length and the headroom are the same measurement, so leg length divided by headroom is one over one, which is one. Every other leg you ever calculate will have a factor bigger than one, because any lean adds tension. Some folks will guess a half, which would mean a vertical leg somehow carries less than the weight it holds, and that is not physical. ## Slide 15

Step three ties it together, and it is a multiplication. Tension equals the share times the load angle factor. The share is the weight portion, the factor is the angle penalty, and their product is the real tension in the leg. That tension is what you take to the sling chart, not the share. The difference between the two numbers is exactly what the angle cost you, and it is the number a rigger leaves on the table when they size off the share and forget the factor. Once you have the tension, you check it against the sling's rated capacity with its design factor, the same way you would any load. Three steps: factor, share, multiply. Rigid versus flexible is the distinction that makes the four-leg bridle dangerous, and it is worth one more pass.
