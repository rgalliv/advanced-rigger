Section 1

Friction Fundamentals

What Friction Is

Friction is the resistance to sliding between two surfaces pressed together -- the grip between the load and whatever it rests on.

It is not the weight itself and not speed. It is the resistance you must overcome to slide the load.

The Coefficient of Friction

The coefficient of friction (the Greek letter mu) is a plain number for a pair of surfaces: steel on steel, steel on wood, rubber on concrete.

Higher number = more resistance = more force to slide. Greased surfaces are low; rough dry surfaces are high.

Static vs Kinetic Friction

Two kinds of friction matter when you move a load:

  • Static (breakaway) friction -- holds a still load; you fight it to start motion
  • Kinetic (sliding) friction -- acts once the load is moving; it is lower

Because static is higher, a load breaks loose suddenly and can lunge -- be ready for it.

It takes more force to start a load sliding than to keep it moving because:

Sizing the Sliding Force

On a level surface, the force to slide a load is:

Force = coefficient of friction (mu) x Weight

Example: mu = 0.5 and a 10,000 lb load -> about 5,000 lb of pull (plus a bit more to break it loose).

This sizes your winch, rigging, and anchor.

Rolling vs Sliding

Rolling Beats Sliding

Rolling resistance (rollers, wheels, dollies) is far lower than sliding friction -- often just a fraction.

That is why heavy loads go on pipe rollers, dollies, or skates instead of being skidded. A load you can barely drag will roll with a modest push.

Compared with sliding a load, rolling it on rollers or wheels usually takes:

Rollers, Dollies, and Air Skates

Load-moving equipment reduces resistance in different amounts:

  • Pipe rollers, dollies, skates -- cut resistance way down
  • Air skates (air bearings) -- float the load on a film of air, nearly frictionless (need a smooth, sealed floor)

The less resistance, the more the load must be actively controlled.

A Load on Rollers Wants to Run

A load on rollers has almost no resistance to rolling -- it will move from the slightest push, bump, or unseen slope.

Always chock or restrain a load on rollers whenever it is not being deliberately moved. Rollers do not hold a load; they free it to move.

Reducing the Force to Move

To make a heavy load easier to move on the level:

  • Roll it instead of sliding it -- the biggest win
  • If it must slide, lubricate the surfaces to drop the coefficient

Do not add weight on top (raises friction) or rely on speed -- both work against you.

Section 3

Inclined Planes

The Ramp as a Simple Machine

An inclined plane lets you raise a load with less force than a vertical lift -- by moving it over a longer distance along the slope.

Force goes down, distance goes up; the work is about the same. That is the bargain every simple machine makes.

Knowledge Check

An inclined plane (ramp) works as a simple machine by:

Ideal Mechanical Advantage of a Ramp

The ideal mechanical advantage of a ramp:

Ideal MA = ramp length (along slope) / vertical height

A 12 ft ramp rising 3 ft = 4:1 -- about a quarter of the weight to push up (ignoring friction).

Longer and shallower (for a given height) = more advantage.

Knowledge Check

The ideal mechanical advantage of a ramp is:

Force to Go Up a Ramp

Moving a load up an incline fights two forces at once:

  • The gravity component along the slope (pulling the load back down)
  • The friction between load and ramp

Flat ground has only friction. A ramp adds the gravity component on top -- so up-ramp always needs more pull.

Knowledge Check

The force needed to move a load UP an inclined plane must overcome:

Steeper Ramps Cost More

As a ramp gets steeper, the gravity component along the slope grows:

  • More force needed to move the load up
  • Stronger tendency for the load to run back down

A long, shallow ramp is easier and safer than a short, steep one for the same height.

Lowering: The Runaway Hazard

Lowering a load down a ramp must be actively controlled.

Gravity now drives the load down the slope. If the gravity component beats the friction holding it, the load runs away downhill -- faster than intended, dragging the rigging and anyone below.

Never just release it. Never trust friction alone to stop it.

Section 4

Drifting, Anchors, and Control

Drifting a Load

Drifting is moving a suspended load horizontally using two or more hoists -- tightening some and slackening others so the weight passes from one to the next.

It walks a load sideways through the air when a single straight pick will not reach. A controlled hand-off, not a swing.

Knowledge Check

Drifting a load means:

High-Risk Conditions

A load-moving or drifting job climbs in risk when:

  • The weight is unknown or unverified
  • The center of gravity is above the attachment points (wants to flip)
  • The load is skidded or skated up or down an inclined plane

Anchors and the Snatch-Block Resultant

When a winch or tugger pulls through a snatch block, the anchor (deadman) must be rated for the resultant force on the block -- which can reach about twice the line pull at a hard reversal.

Same vector rule as the blocks module. Rate the deadman to the resultant, not the bare line pull.

Control the Load -- Never Trust Friction

Actively control any load that could move on its own. Keep people clear -- out from under it and never downhill of it.

Never trust friction as a brake: it is variable and can let go the instant it breaks from static to sliding, or if a surface gets wet or greased.

The Hardware Rules Still Apply

However you move the load -- sliding, rolling, ramping, drifting -- the blocks, shackles, and slings in the system must still be:

  • Rated and inspected to their own standards (blocks, B30.26 hardware, B30.9 slings)
  • Sized so the weakest piece governs the whole system

Moving a load does not suspend the rules that protect a lift.

Worked Example: Sliding Force

Put the level-surface rule to work:

Force = mu x Weight

A 4,000 lb load, mu = 0.5 -> about 2,000 lb of pull (plus a bit more to break it loose from rest).

Since mu is less than 1, the sliding force is less than the weight.
