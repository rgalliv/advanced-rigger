## Slide 4

Let us build a wire rope from the inside out, because every kind of damage you will inspect for makes sense once you see the structure. The smallest piece is a single wire. Lay a bunch of wires together and you get a strand. Lay several strands around a central core and you have the rope. So it is wires into strands, strands around a core. That core is doing real work -- it supports the strands and keeps the rope round under load. It comes two ways: an independent wire rope core, a little rope inside the rope, which is stronger and handles heat better; or a fiber core, which is more flexible but gives up heat and crush resistance. When we talk later about a rope losing diameter or getting crushed, this core is what has failed. On a job I watched a fiber-core sling get flattened under a load edge that an independent wire rope core might have shrugged off. ## Slide 6

Now the ends. A termination is how the rope ends and attaches -- the eye you hook into, the socket poured onto the end, the fitting that connects it to the world. Here is the part that surprises people: the termination is almost always the weakest point of the whole assembly. The rope body might be good for its full published strength, but the way you end it decides how much of that you actually get. So we rate terminations by efficiency -- the percent of the rope's strength the connection keeps. A hundred percent efficient end gives you the whole rope; a lower one gives you less. That is why a good rope on a bad end is a weak assembly. When you inspect, you inspect the termination as hard as the rope. I have watched a strong rope let go right at a sloppy end connection while the body never even strained. [PAUSE / ASK] Why is the end usually the weakest part?

## Slide 8

Terminations are not equal, and here is the rough ranking. ## Slide 10

## Slide 12

The splice has a hard minimum, and it is the kind of number an inspector will check. A hand-tucked eye splice in wire rope needs at least three full tucks of each strand woven back into the body. Fewer than three and the eye can creep and pull out under load. The tucks are what let the eye grip itself. You cover or blunt the protruding strand ends so they do not slice a hand, and you size the eye so that when it goes over a load or pin, the included angle at the splice stays sixty degrees or less -- a wider eye angle pries the splice open. Fiber ropes ask for even more tucks, but for wire rope the floor you never drop below is three. I have watched an inspector count tucks on every eye before a single hook went on. ## Slide 17

Broken wires are not the only thing that ends a rope -- damage to its shape does too, because once the strands are distorted they stop sharing the load evenly. Learn these three by sight. A kink is a permanent tight bend that has deformed the rope for good. A birdcage is where the strands spring outward like the bars of a cage, and it usually comes from shock loading or a sudden release of tension. Crushing or flattening is when the rope loses its round cross section, often from being run over or pinched. Any one of the three takes the sling out of service on the spot, because you cannot work the damage back out -- it is permanent. Some of these happen in a single bad moment. ## Slide 21

Two more removals to lock in. First, heat. Enough heat changes the rope steel and cooks the lubricant deep inside, and it does it without an obvious mark, so a sling that has been exposed to real heat comes out for evaluation and, in practice, out of service -- you treat its strength as unknown. And do not throw water on a hot sling to cool it, because quenching can make the metal damage worse. Second, the end fittings. The socket, the thimble, the hardware in the eye -- if any of that is cracked, bent, badly worn, or corroded, the assembly is done, because the load runs straight through those fittings.
