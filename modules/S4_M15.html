Section 1

What Center of Gravity Actually Is

Center of Gravity Defined

The center of gravity is the single point in a load where its weight is evenly balanced in every direction.

  • Support the load at that point and it will balance, with no tendency to rotate
  • Suspend the load and it will always come to rest with that point directly under the hook
  • It is a property of the load itself, set by shape and material distribution
  • It exists whether or not anybody has calculated it

Shorthand you will hear on site: CG, and occasionally COG. Both mean the same point.

The CG Is a Point in Space

The center of gravity does not have to fall on solid material.

  • A steel ring balances at the center of the circle -- in open air
  • A U-shaped or L-shaped weldment balances outside its own material
  • A pipe spool bent back on itself can balance in the gap
  • An open frame or skid balances in the space the frame surrounds

This is why you cannot always mark the CG with paint. You can often only mark the vertical line it falls on.

The Hook Must Be Over the CG

A load hanging from a single hoist line will always settle with its center of gravity directly beneath the hook.

  • Hook over the CG: the load rises level and hangs quiet
  • Hook offset from the CG: the load swings and rotates until it corrects itself
  • The correction happens the instant the load goes free of the ground
  • An offset hookup also loads sling legs unevenly

The load line should also be plumb. Side pull introduces stresses the equipment was not designed to carry.

Knowledge Check

A load's center of gravity can be located in open air, outside the material of the load itself.

What an Offset Hookup Costs You

When the hook is not over the CG:

  • Swing. The load accelerates sideways as it lifts free
  • Rotation. The load turns to bring the CG under the hook
  • Uneven leg loading. One leg carries more than its calculated share
  • Loss of control. Tag line handlers are reacting instead of guiding
  • Struck-by exposure. Anyone in the swing path is at risk

Section 2

Finding the Center of Gravity

Order of Confidence

  1. Documented data. Manufacturer drawings, data plates, general arrangement drawings, shipping documents. Highest confidence.
  2. Calculation. Geometry for uniform loads; the moment method for composite loads. Reliable when the inputs are good.
  3. Weighing. Scales or load cells. Gives real numbers for the load as it sits right now, contents included.
  4. Trial lift observation. Verification, not determination. Confirms or corrects what you already worked out.

ASME B30.5 places the duty to obtain the load weight and its approximate CG on the rigger.

Symmetric and Uniform Loads

When a load is one material, uniform in thickness, and regular in shape, the CG sits at the geometric center.

  • Rectangular plate: center in both directions
  • Solid round bar or pipe of uniform wall: mid-length, on the axis
  • Cube or rectangular solid: center in all three directions

The assumption breaks the moment anything is not uniform. Cutouts, attached components, tapered sections, thicker end plates, internal bracing, and residual contents all move the CG away from the geometric center.

The Plumb-Bob Method

For an irregular load you can pick up and hang freely, the plumb-bob method reads the CG directly off the suspended load.

  1. Suspend the load from one attachment point and let it settle
  2. Drop a plumb line from the hook -- the CG lies on that line. Mark it (Line A)
  3. Re-hang from a different point and drop a second line (Line B)
  4. The CG is where Line A and Line B cross

Best used in the shop, before a piece goes to the field: the load must hang completely free with no ground or obstruction contact, the hoist must be rated for the full weight, and wind throws it off -- so do it indoors or in calm air. For field-installed equipment that cannot be re-suspended, the trial lift or the two-scale method is more practical.

The Moment Method: The Idea

A moment is a turning effect. It equals weight multiplied by distance from a chosen reference point.

Moment = Weight × Distance

Think of a seesaw. A light child sitting far from the pivot balances a heavy child sitting close to it, because what balances is not weight alone and not distance alone -- it is the two multiplied together.

A composite load is just several children on the same seesaw. The CG is the point where all their moments balance.

The Moment Method: The Procedure

  1. Pick a reference point and stay with it. An end of the load, a machined face, a bolt line -- any point works, as long as every measurement comes off the same one.
  2. Break the load into components whose individual weights you know or can estimate.
  3. Measure each component's distance from the reference point to that component's own center of gravity.
  4. Multiply each weight by its distance to get each moment.
  5. Add all the moments together.
  6. Divide the total moment by the total weight.
CG distance = (Sum of all moments) ÷ (Total weight)

Repeat along a second axis to locate the CG in two dimensions.

Worked Example: Two-Component Skid

Given: a 20 ft skid, 8,000 lb total.
Component A: 6,000 lb, centered 4 ft from the left end.
Component B: 2,000 lb, centered 16 ft from the left end.

Reference point: left end of the skid

Moment A = 6,000 lb × 4 ft = 24,000 lb-ft
Moment B = 2,000 lb × 16 ft = 32,000 lb-ft

Total moment = 24,000 + 32,000 = 56,000 lb-ft
Total weight = 6,000 + 2,000 = 8,000 lb

CG = 56,000 ÷ 8,000 = 7 ft from the left end

Sanity check: the CG landed closer to the heavy component, which is what it should do. If your answer drifts toward the light end, you made an error.

The Two-Scale Weighing Method

Support the load at two known points and read the weight at each. You get both the total weight and the CG location.

Total weight = Left reading + Right reading

Distance from left support = (Right reading × Span) ÷ Total weight
  • Use calibrated scales or load cells under cribbing at each support
  • The load must be level and resting only on the two supports
  • The CG sits nearer the support carrying the greater share
  • Reflects the load as it sits now, including contents

Worked Example: Two-Scale

Given: a 30 ft beam on two scales, 30 ft apart.
Left scale: 4,000 lb · Right scale: 2,000 lb

Total weight = 4,000 + 2,000 = 6,000 lb

Distance from left = (2,000 × 30) ÷ 6,000
                  = 60,000 ÷ 6,000
                  = 10 ft from the left end

Sanity check: the left scale carries twice the right, so the CG should be twice as close to the left. 10 ft vs 20 ft. It checks.

Section 3

Verifying in the Field

The Trial Lift

Raise the load only until it is just clear of the ground. Stop. Watch.

  • All personnel positioned clear before the load breaks contact
  • Lift slowly and stop within a few inches of the ground
  • Observe whether the load rises level or tilts
  • If it tilts: set it down and reposition the rigging
  • Repeat until the load rises level
  • If it will not come level, stop and re-examine the plan

The trial lift is the cheapest information you will get. At two inches, a bad hookup costs you time. At ten feet, it costs more.

The direction of the tilt tells you where the CG is.

Load rolls in the slingsPick points are below the CGSet down; re-rig above the CG

The low end is the heavy end. The most common field error is reading this backward and moving the hook the wrong direction.

Correcting an Out-of-Level Load

On a fixed-attachment bridle, the load tilts toward the heavy end. Three legitimate corrections, in the order you will usually reach for them:

  1. Shorten the sling on the low (heavy) side -- this lifts the heavy end up to level. (Equivalently, lengthen the high/light side.)
  2. Move the attachment toward the heavy end, so the hook ends up over the CG.
  3. Adjust effective leg length with turnbuckles or chain lever hoists when the attachment points are fixed.

Not acceptable: lengthening the low side (drops it further); holding a load level with tag lines; adding counterweight; adjusting rigging while the load is suspended; increasing hoist speed to reduce apparent swing.

When leg lengths differ, leg tensions differ too. Recheck your sling capacities against the new geometry.

Section 4

Loads That Fight You

Liquid and Loose Contents

A partially filled or loosely loaded container does not have a fixed CG.

  • Liquid runs to the low side when the load tilts, moving the CG with it
  • Acceleration, stopping, and swinging all drive the contents
  • The shift can be sudden and self-reinforcing: tilt drives flow, flow increases tilt
  • Loose bulk material, unsecured tooling, and stacked parts behave the same way

Preferred controls: empty the container, or fill it completely. If it must be lifted partly full, use baffles or internal restraint, move slowly, avoid abrupt starts and stops, and treat the CG as a range rather than a point.

High CG Loads and Tipping

Tall, narrow loads with the weight concentrated high are stable in the air and unstable on the ground.

  • Suspended from above, the load hangs stably
  • The hazard arrives at set-down, when the load takes its own weight
  • Uneven ground, settling cribbing, or a soft spot starts the tip
  • Once past the tipping point, the load's own weight completes the fall

Controls: prepare and level the set-down area in advance; use adequate blocking with sufficient bearing area; keep tag lines attached until the load is stable and restrained; brace or guy tall loads before releasing the rigging.

When the CG Cannot Be Determined

Sometimes the information is not there. OSHA addresses this directly for assembly and disassembly work at 29 CFR 1926.1404(h)(6) in Subpart CC, and the principle applies to rigging generally.

  • The CG must be identified where it is necessary for the method used to maintain stability
  • Where information is insufficient to identify it accurately, measures designed to prevent dangerous movement resulting from an inaccurate identification must be used

What those measures look like: a slow controlled trial lift with everyone clear; tag lines rigged before lift-off; increased exclusion zone; restraint against rotation; a conservative CG assumption with margin; a second lifting point available; and a documented stop point if the load does not behave as expected.

Documenting the CG

The center of gravity is lift plan information, not a number that lives in one person's head.

  • Record the CG location and how it was determined
  • Record the total weight and its source
  • Mark the CG line on the load where it can be seen, when practical
  • Communicate it in the pre-lift briefing so the whole crew knows
  • Under ASME B30.5 the rigger ensures the weight and approximate CG have been obtained; the lift director ensures the operator is informed of the weight

If the load is picked again later by a different crew, your documentation is what they inherit.
